The Curious Case of Balagere T Junction

It passes 6% of its rush hour. Nothing can be widened.

It is not a crossroads. Two side roads meet the Panathur–Varthur road 12.6 m apart, there is no traffic light, and two give-way U-turns do the work a signalised junction would. 339 simulations later, three changes — none of them construction — take it from 6% to 92%.

01The problem

Two T junctions 12.6 m apart, carrying 5,743 vehicles an hour

Balagere sits on the Panathur–Varthur road in East Bangalore. Kundalahalli joins from the north and Sarjapur from the south — but not at the same place. The two side roads meet the main road 12.6 m apart, so the site is a staggered pair of T junctions, not a crossroads.

That stagger is the origin of the problem. From Kundalahalli you cannot turn across to Panathur, because the opposite side road is 12.6 m further along. You join the main road and double back instead — which is why the junction depends on U-turns.

At rush hour 5,743 vehicles an hour arrive and it passes 6%. Rebuilding works — a proper crossroads with three lanes per approach simulates at 99% served — but construction was ruled out. That leaves the signal controller, road signs and road markings.

02The baseline

Today: no signal, two U-turns, 6% served

There is no traffic light. Every junction is give-way controlled. A median cut lets Sarjapur–Kundalahalli traffic cross the Panathur–Varthur road, and two U-turns handle the movements that cannot be made directly.

Kundalahalli→Panathur and Kundalahalli→Sarjapur both need one U-turn. Sarjapur→Varthur needs the other. Both must yield, and both sit on single-lane approaches — so one vehicle waiting for a gap stops everyone behind it. 1,667 veh/h, 29% of all demand, goes through those two U-turns.

What is wrong

  • It gridlocks off-peak too. At 38% of peak demand it still serves under a quarter, with 110 gridlocks. The U-turns break it at any load.
  • Vehicles are stationary 94% of the journey. 112 had to be teleported out of deadlocks — with no signal, nothing breaks the cycle.
  • All four approaches fail equally (5–10%). This is not a split to be retimed. The junction is not passing traffic.
03Methodology

One demand set, 339 runs, one variable at a time

Eclipse SUMO 1.27.1 — models every vehicle individually, not traffic as flow.

  • Identical demand in every run — same vehicles, types and departure times. Trips carry an origin and destination, not a fixed route, so each design routes the same traffic over its own geometry.
  • Shared settings — fixed seed, one-second steps, and a 30-minute limit on waiting to enter before a trip counts as unserved.
  • Service measured per approach, by matching each trip to its completion record. A total alone hides an abandoned arm (section 05).
  • Every signal plan checked for validity against the junction's conflict matrix. Without this, two-phase plans report throughput that cannot physically happen (section 05).

Fleet: 53% two-wheelers, 33% cars, 8% auto-rickshaws, 3% trucks, 1.5% buses. Biggest movement: Panathur↔Varthur through traffic, 2,316 veh/h — 40% of demand, with no destination at the junction.

04The solution

Ban the U-turns, signalise, mark two lanes in the existing 6 m

Each approach already has 6 m of sealed carriageway marked as one lane. Nothing here needs new tarmac.

Median works, a signal, paint, one diversion sign. No new road, and the junction serves its rush hour.

Signal timing alone — flat at 52%

98 programs: two, three and four phases, cycles 50–240 s, fixed and actuated. All landed between 50.4% and 51.8%. Actuated control was equal or worse — with every approach saturated there is no gap for it to detect. This is what redirected the study away from the controller.

Two-phase plans — impossible, then unfair

Running two approaches together is the usual way to gain capacity. Written naively it reported much higher throughput, because two conflicting streams had both been given a full green into the same lane with neither yielding (Lane '-E3_0' is targeted by 2 'G'-links). Rebuilt correctly, it still led on the total — until split by approach.

PlanTotalPanathurKundalahalli VarthurSarjapurWorst
Two-phase 52.0% 89%6%90%7% 5.8%
Four-phase 47.4%49%44%43%55% 43.0%

It wins the total by serving Panathur and Varthur near 90% and abandoning Kundalahalli and Sarjapur at 6 and 7%. Pairing conflicting approaches forces the weaker to give way, and giving way to saturated traffic yields nothing. Optimising the headline number alone would have cut two neighbourhoods off.

Still true after re-marking: the best two-phase plan leaves Sarjapur at 44% with 49 gridlocks, against every arm above 85% and none for four phases.

Banning the worst turns — disconnects a neighbourhood

Two right turns each yield to six or seven movements. Removing either returns no valid route — there is no other path from Kundalahalli to Panathur.

Give-way reassignment — no effect

Six priority schemes moved service from 52.0% to 52.1%. The blockage was the 15.5 m segment's length, not the rule governing it.

Signals on the other two junctions — worse

50.1% against 52.1%. Three controllers 20–37 m apart cannot be coordinated without one queue spilling into the next.

Extra green for the starved approach — worse overall

Kundalahalli given 40% and 80% more green reaches 55–57% and pushes Sarjapur and Varthur down, so the worst arm worsens. Storage was binding, not green time.

A two-wheeler lane — much worse

A 4.0 m general lane plus a 2.0 m motorcycle-only lane serves 67.9% against 92.5% for two equal lanes: it denies a third of the road to the 47% of traffic that is not a two-wheeler. Two ordinary lanes let them filter anyway, which lifts the recommendation to 95.1%.

07Limits

What to measure before building this

Simulation on estimated counts: strong evidence on which option wins, weak evidence on absolute numbers. Gaps in order of importance.

The traffic counts are estimates, not a survey

The count data says it was calibrated from IRC:106 benchmarks and Bangalore modal-split studies, not measured here. Because every option was tested against the same demand, the ranking holds — it is the same at 60%, 80%, 100% and 120% of peak. The percentages and the 4,591 veh/h ceiling inherit the estimate's uncertainty. A turning-movement count at this junction is the highest-value thing to add.

Saturation flow is uncalibrated

Following distances were set by judgement — 0.5 m for two-wheelers to 1.5 m for buses — because the source data omits them. The simulator's 2.5 m default would put throughput well below what Indian urban roads achieve. Identical across all 339 runs, so they cannot bias the comparison, but they move the ceiling.

Three-metre lanes are narrow

Fine for a fleet that is 53% two-wheelers and only 4.5% buses or trucks, but the design must be checked against local standards for how long vehicles track through a turn. This is the main delivery risk.

Pedestrians are not modelled

A signalised junction on this corridor needs a pedestrian phase, and the recommended 180-second cycle has no room budgeted for one. It has to come out of the existing green time, not extend the cycle — see the cliff in the recommendation.

Re-marking slightly shortens the internal segments

A wider approach eats slightly more road at the junction mouth, so internal segments shorten by 1–3 m — the critical one from 15.5 m to 12.8 m. The design therefore wins despite marginally less storage, not because of more.

Lane discipline is modelled optimistically

Two-wheeler filtering was modelled and is worth about 2.6 points, but vehicles are still assumed to largely keep to lanes. Real behaviour is looser, which helps narrow layouts most — so this probably understates the benefit.

How to check this

Scripted end to end: demand, network variants, the run harness, the figures and this page are all regenerated from source, so no number is typed by hand. All 339 simulations run in a few minutes on a laptop.
